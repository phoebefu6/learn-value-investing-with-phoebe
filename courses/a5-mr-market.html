Learn Value Investing with Phoebe · Owner session 5 of 6

Mr Market quotes, you decide

Four sessions in, you have a range for what Halden Tiles is worth, a ceiling you would pay, and an edge to what you know. Tonight the price comes back, and it comes back every day. Benjamin Graham pictured it as a business partner who shouts a new offer each morning, cheerful one day and miserable the next; Buffett retold the picture in his 1987 letter. The skill is not predicting his next mood. It is knowing, before he speaks, which of his offers you would take, which you would ignore, and how to tell a change in his temper from a change in the business. No market prices; every quote on the page is constructed.

Part 0

Where we start

Owner session 3 tested one price, the cousin's asking price of 10.0 million for the whole of Halden. A single price is easy to keep in its place. A price that changes every day is harder, because each new number arrives with a feeling attached: relief when it rises, dread when it falls, and a quiet urge to treat the latest number as the truth. Tonight is about that urge. You will meet the partner who produces the numbers, sort his offers into three zones you draw in advance, and watch two weeks of his constructed quotes swing while the business underneath them does not.

Part 1 · covers the allegory and what a quote is

The partner who quotes every day

5 min live

Imagine you own a slice of a private business together with an obliging partner. Every single day, without being asked, he names a price at which he will either buy your share or sell you his. Some days he is elated and can see only good things ahead, so his price is high. Other days he is gloomy and can see only trouble, so his price is low. He does not mind in the least if you ignore him; tomorrow he will be back with a new number. That is the whole allegory, and the business never appears in it.

LiveThe quote is information about his mood, not about the business3 min▶

Look at what changed between day 1 and day 4 in the figure. Halden sold the same tiles from the same three showrooms to the same builders. Its owner earnings were still about 1.00 million a year. The only thing that moved was the partner's offer, from 10.0 million for the whole to 16.6. That move tells you something real, but what it tells you is about him: on day 4 he felt good. It does not tell you that the business became worth 6.6 million more over a long weekend.

This is the reading Buffett gives the allegory in his 1987 letter. The partner's offers swing with his emotional state, not with the business, and the letter's line for how to treat them is the one to keep: "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you." A quote serves you when it hands you a chance to trade at a price far from your value. It guides you, wrongly, when you let it tell you what the value is.

Self-studyYou may ignore him at no cost3 min▶

The most underrated feature of the partner is that he does not mind being ignored. You are never obliged to accept his offer, and declining it costs you nothing that you own. Your slice of Halden still earns its share of the owners' cash whether or not you looked at the quote today. That is the asymmetry that makes the allegory useful: he must quote, you need not answer.

The cost of ignoring him is only an opportunity cost on the rare days his offer is far from your value, and those are the days your written zones flag (Part 2). On every other day, not answering is the correct and complete response. People who feel they must act on every quote are paying, in attention and in mistakes, for a service that was offered for free.

In a listed company the partner is the market itself: a stranger's bid appears every few seconds whether you want one or not. Halden is private, so the page makes the cousin play him, constructed, for two weeks. The behaviour that matters is the same either way.

Real world

Buffett, 1987 letter. Buffett credited the allegory to Ben Graham, whom he called his friend and teacher, and told it to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders to explain how an owner should treat a price that changes every day. The letter does not name a book. Graham's own telling is usually traced to chapter 8 of The Intelligent Investor (reported; the books were not read at source for these pages, so Graham is paraphrased, not quoted). The version on this page follows Buffett's 1987 wording, read at source on berkshirehathaway.com.

Part 2 · covers using a quote against your range

Use the quote, do not obey it

5 min live

A quote can only serve you if you already know what you think the business is worth. Owner session 2 gave Halden a range of 8.2 to 15.8 million for the whole, at a 10 percent discount rate. Owner session 3 set a ceiling of 8.50, a one-third margin under the base view of 12.75. Those two pieces of paper turn every quote into one of three plain responses, and you can decide all three before the partner opens his mouth.

LiveWhat to do on a gloomy day3 min▶

On a gloomy day the partner's offer falls, and the falling number feels like a verdict. The discipline is a short sequence, done in order. First, ask whether anything happened to the business: a customer lost, a cost risen, a rival opened, a person gone. If not, this is mood. Second, find the zone. A gloomy quote that is still above your ceiling of 8.50 is in the middle zone, and the response is to ignore it. Only a quote below the ceiling, like day 8's 7.8 in the figure above, puts you in the zone where buying from him is worth considering.

"Consider" is the honest word. It means: check that your range still stands, then decide whether to take his offer at your ceiling or below it. It does not mean the low quote proves you right. Gloom can be wrong, and it can also be early, which is why the next card insists on the range being written before the quote.

Self-studyWhy your own value has to be written down first3 min▶

If you have no written range when the quote arrives, the quote fills the gap. It is the only number in the room, so it quietly becomes your estimate of value, and from then on you are no longer using his price: you are obeying it. When he is elated your "value" rises with him, and when he is gloomy it falls with him. You end up buying after his good days and selling after his bad ones, which is the exact opposite of what the zones are for.

A written range breaks that pull because it was made before the quote and from different material: the owner earnings, the three views and the discount rate from Owner session 2. The sell floor works the same way. Halden's floor here is the high value of 15.8; some owners set it higher still, for the same reason a ceiling sits below the base. Either is a judgment, but it has to be your judgment, written in calm weather. The pull of the first number you see is the anchoring trap, and Owner session 6 takes it apart.

Real world

Halden, the cousin's 10.0 revisited, constructed. In Owner session 3 the cousin's asking price of 1.00 million for 10 percent, or 10.0 for the whole, sat above your ceiling of 8.50, and the margin against the bear view was negative. Put the same number on the strip and the reading is calmer: 10.0 is inside your range, in the middle zone. You would not buy at it, and you would not sell at it either. It is neither an opportunity nor a threat; it is simply a number you can let pass.

Part 3 · covers temperament and mood versus news

Temperament is the scarce skill

5 min live

The zones are arithmetic, and anyone can draw them. Keeping to them on the day the partner is shouting is not arithmetic. Buffett's 1987 letter puts the condition plainly: the game is worth playing only if you understand the business and can value it better than Mr. Market does. If you cannot, his mood will become yours, because you have nothing steadier to hold on to. The mechanism below is where temperament actually lives.

LiveRules written in calm weather3 min▶

Nobody writes good rules on a day the partner is shouting. The rules that hold are the ones set on a quiet day, when no quote is pulling at you: your range, your ceiling, your sell floor, and one more line most people skip, which is what news would make you revisit the range. Write all four on one card and date it.

Then, when the quote arrives, your only job is to read the card. That is a smaller, steadier task than deciding from scratch. The card does not stop you changing your mind; it insists that you change it for a reason about the business, written down, and not because a number moved. If you cannot fill in the card for a business, that is Owner session 4's signal: it sits outside your edge, and Buffett's condition, understanding the business better than the partner does, is not met.

Self-studyA business change or a mood change: how to tell3 min▶

Ask two questions of any big move in the quote. Is there a new fact? Something about the customers (a builder leaves, or a large new one arrives), the costs (an importer raises prices, rent goes up), the rivals (a new showroom opens nearby, direct online ordering spreads) or the people (the cousin steps back, a key salesperson leaves). Would that fact change one of your inputs? The spend to stand still, the growth path in one of your three views, or how much weight you give the bear view.

If both answers are yes, you have a business change: revisit the range, on paper, using the input that moved, and only then look at the zones again. If there is no new fact, you have a mood change, however large the move: go straight to the zones. Most big moves fail the first question. A fact can also arrive on a quiet day with no move at all, and it still deserves a revisit; the quote is not the only messenger for news.

Real world

Buffett's condition, 1987 letter. The same letter that introduces the partner ends the passage with a condition on who should play at all: the game is worth playing only if you understand the business and can value it better than Mr. Market does. That turns the allegory back on the earlier sessions. The zones only work for an owner who did the work of Owner sessions 1 to 4. Without that work, the quote is the best information you have, and his mood has no rival.

Build-along

Two weeks of quotes, constructed

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code and no market prices. For two weeks the cousin plays Mr. Market and offers, each trading day, to buy your 10 percent or sell you hers at a single price. Every quote below is constructed, invented to make the reasoning visible; your numbers will differ, and if you run the exercise on a business you know, the quotes will be ones you make up too. Write your card first, then read the days one at a time, as they would arrive.

Step 1 · Write your range first. Before you look at a single quote, copy your range from Owner session 2 onto the card. If you skip this step, the first quote you read becomes your range by default.

★ Step 1 · the range, from Owner session 2Halden, whole business, at 10 percent: 8.2 to 15.8 million Base view: 12.75 million Written on a quiet day, before any quote. Dated.

Step 2 · Set your buy ceiling and your sell floor. The ceiling comes from Owner session 3. The sell floor is new tonight: the price above which you would consider selling your slice to him. Using your high value is one honest choice; a higher floor is another. Write the reason next to it.

★ Step 2 · the two edges, constructedBuy ceiling: 8.50 for the whole (one-third margin under the base of 12.75) = 0.85 for 10 percent Sell floor: 15.8 for the whole (my high value; above it he pays for my bull case) = 1.58 for 10 percent In between: ignore him. No action, no cost.

Step 3 · Read each day's quote and put it on the same scale. The cousin quotes for your 10 percent. Multiply by ten so every number sits on the same scale as your range. Do this one day at a time, in order, as if you could not see tomorrow.

★ Step 3 · scaling, constructedDay 1: 1.00 for 10 percent x 10 = 10.0 for the whole Day 4: 1.66 for 10 percent x 10 = 16.6 for the whole Day 8: 0.78 for 10 percent x 10 = 7.8 for the whole (the full ten days are in the table above)

Step 4 · Mark the zone. Compare each whole-business quote with your two edges. Below 8.50 is the buy zone, above 15.8 the sell zone, everything else the middle. Mark the zone only; do not decide anything yet.

★ Step 4 · zones, constructedDays 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10: between 8.50 and 15.8 middle zone, ignore Day 4 (16.6): above 15.8 sell zone, consider Day 8 (7.8): below 8.50 buy zone, consider

Step 5 · Write what news, if any, came that day. A new fact about customers, costs, rivals or people. Most days the honest entry is "none". When there is news, ask whether it moves one of your inputs, and if it does, revisit the range on paper before acting on any zone.

★ Step 5 · mood or news, constructedDay 4: no news. A mood. Sell zone, but only because he is elated. Day 7: news. A third builder starts ordering direct online. A fact about customers: it makes the bear view more likely. Revisit the range before day 8's quote means anything. Day 8: no news. If the range still stands after the day 7 review, the buy zone holds. If the bear view now weighs more, my ceiling may need to come down. Decide on paper first.

Step 6 · Tally the days a decision was warranted. Count the days outside the middle zone, and the days with news. Then write one sentence on what the tally says about how often a daily quote deserves your attention.

★ Step 6 · the tally, constructedDays in the middle zone: 8 of 10 (no action needed) Days a decision was warranted: 2 of 10 (days 4 and 8) Days with news: 1 of 10 (day 7, and its quote was in the middle) "Both of his extreme offers came on days with no news. The one real fact arrived on an ordinary-looking day."

Keep the card as a standing rule. Range, ceiling, floor, and the news that would make you revisit, written in calm weather and dated. Read the card when the quote arrives; change the card only for a written reason about the business.

Homework

Before Owner session 6

30 min
  1. Write a calm-weather card for a business you know: range, buy ceiling, sell floor, and one sentence naming the news that would make you revisit the range. Date it.
  2. Invent ten days of quotes for it, labelled constructed, with at least one elated day and one gloomy day, and one day with a real-sounding piece of news. Zone each day and tally it as in step 6.
  3. Sort five headlines about any businesses you follow into mood or news, using the two questions from Part 3: is there a new fact, and would it change one of your inputs?
  4. Notice one moment this week when a number you saw changed how you felt about something you own or use, and write one line on whether anything about the thing itself had changed. Owner session 6 is about where that pull comes from.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Owner session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In Buffett's 1987 telling of Graham's allegory, what is Mr. Market?

The allegory's point is that his quotes reflect his temper, elated or gloomy, rather than the business, and that you are free to ignore him; he will be back tomorrow. His offer is a chance you may take, never your estimate of value.

2 · Your written range for Halden (constructed) is 8.2 to 15.8 million, your ceiling 8.50 and your sell floor 15.8. Today's quote, scaled to the whole, is 11.4. What do you do?

Inside the range there is not enough gap between his price and your value to pay you for being wrong, in either direction. Buying below the base without a margin repeats the mistake Owner session 3 warned about, and moving the range to fit the quote is exactly how a quote becomes your value.

3 · The partner's quote drops by a fifth in two days. How do you tell whether this is a mood change or a business change?

The size of a move tells you about his temper, not its cause. In the build-along both extreme quotes came on days with no news, while the one real fact arrived on a day the quote looked ordinary. A new fact that moves an input means revisiting the range on paper; no new fact means going straight to the zones.

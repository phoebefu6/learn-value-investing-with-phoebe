The edge of what you know

Owner session 3 gave you a margin of safety against errors in your estimate. Tonight is about the error no margin can cover: valuing a business you do not actually understand. You draw a circle around the businesses you can explain, test its edge with four plain questions, and learn to put everything else in a tray marked too hard. The tray is not a failure. It is where most businesses belong, for everyone, and leaving them there costs you nothing.

Part 0

Where we start

Every valuation you have built so far rested on a quiet assumption: that you knew enough about the business to guess its future cash at all. A range from 8.2 to 15.8 million for Halden Tiles (constructed) only means something if the three views behind it were informed guesses rather than stories. When you do not really understand a business, the range is not wide, it is fiction, and a margin of safety measured against fiction protects nothing. So before any number, an owner asks a prior question: is this a business I can judge at all?

Part 1 · covers the circle of competence

A circle you draw, and its edge

5 min live

In his 1996 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, Buffett wrote that an investor does not need to understand every business, or even many of them. You only need to be able to evaluate the ones within your circle of competence. Then comes the line that carries the whole idea: the size of the circle is not very important, but knowing where its boundary runs is vital. A small circle you know the shape of beats a large one you are guessing at.

LiveCompetence means you can say how it earns and what breaks it3 min▶

Most people overrate their circle because they confuse familiarity with understanding. You have heard of a bank, you have an account there, you walk past its branches. None of that tells you how it makes money, what it lends against, or what would sink it. Competence, in the sense the 1996 letter uses, is narrower and more useful: you can say, in plain words, how the business earns its money and what would most plausibly break it.

That test is the first of the four gates from Owner session 1, the one Buffett's 1977 letter put first: a business we can understand. It does most of the filtering. If you cannot pass it, there is no value range to draw, so there is no margin of safety to measure, so there is nothing to decide. The businesses inside your circle are the only ones where the work of Owner sessions 2 and 3 means anything.

Self-studyCircles differ by person, and grow slowly3 min▶

There is no standard circle. A nurse may understand a care-home operator's economics far better than a fund analyst does, because she has seen what staffing shortages do to a ward. A builder may see a tile wholesaler's weak points at a glance. Your circle is drawn from the work you have done, the industries you have watched up close, and the questions you have actually had to answer. That is why it is yours to draw, and why copying someone else's circle is borrowing their competence without their knowledge.

Circles grow, but slowly, and mostly by depth rather than by reading headlines. Learning how one more kind of business earns, spends and fails takes sustained study of that business and its rivals, and the honest measure is the four-question test in Part 2, not a feeling of familiarity. The 1996 letter's point cuts both ways: a small circle is fine, but a circle you think is larger than it is will hurt you exactly where it ends.

Real world

The 1996 letter's two halves. Buffett's 1996 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders sets the circle of competence out as a working rule for ordinary investors, not just professionals. You do not need to be an expert on every company, he wrote; you need to be able to evaluate the ones inside your circle. The weight falls on the boundary: its size is not very important, knowing where it lies is vital. The same letter then turns to what most investors should do instead, which is Part 3.

Part 2 · covers testing the edge

Testing the edge before it tests you

5 min live

The edge is where you know enough to feel confident and not enough to be right. You find it with four questions, each taken straight from the owner's paragraph you wrote in Owner session 1. Can I explain how it makes money? Who are its customers, and why do they come back? What must it spend just to stand still? What would break it in the next five years? Answer each in writing. A business with four written answers is inside. A business with one or two question marks is on the edge. A business where you cannot start the first answer is outside.

LiveThe "I know the product" trap3 min▶

The commonest way to misplace a business is to love its product. You use the app every day, you queue for the trainers, you would never switch phones. That is real knowledge about one customer, you, and it answers part of question 2. It says nothing about question 1 (how the money is actually made, which may be from someone other than you), question 3 (what the business must keep spending to stay in front) or question 4 (what would break it, which a happy customer is the last to see).

Run the test honestly and a product you love often lands on the edge or outside. That is the test working. A business can make something wonderful and still be a poor business to own, because rivals can copy it, because staying ahead eats the profits, or because the customers who love it are not the ones who pay. Why some advantages last and others do not is the economics of moats, taught in Economics, Foundations session 6 ↗; here it only matters as a reason question 4 is hard.

Self-studyWhat you can learn, and how long it takes3 min▶

Not every question mark is equal. Some can be closed with work you control. Halden's question 3 is one of them: ask for the accounts, read them, and check the cousin's 0.50 million against what the showrooms and vans have actually cost over several years. Reading the three statements is its own skill, taught in Finance: Reading the Numbers ↗. It might take a few evenings for a business this simple.

Other question marks are about the world, not the books. Halden's question 4, how fast builders move to ordering direct, cannot be read off any page. You can narrow it by talking to builders and importers, but you may never close it. Sort each question mark into one of three: learnable soon, learnable slowly, or not knowable from here. A business whose open questions are all in the third pile stays on the edge however long you study it, and that is a reason to pass, not a reason to try harder.

Real world

Halden Tiles at the edge, constructed. In Owner session 1 you wrote Halden's owner's paragraph and left two questions open. Owner session 2 built a range of 8.2 to 15.8 million for the whole business at 10 percent, and Owner session 3 measured the cousin's asking price against it. Tonight's test shows what that range rests on: a standing-still figure only the cousin can verify, and a guess about online ordering nobody can yet make. Neither invalidates the range. Both tell you that before you trust its bottom end, there is reading to do and conversations to have.

Part 3 · covers the too-hard tray and the honest default

The too-hard tray and the honest default

5 min live

Once a business is placed, the next move follows from where it sits. Inside the circle: value it, as in Owner session 2, and compare with a price, as in Owner session 3. On the edge: learn what the question marks need, then retest, or pass. Outside: the too-hard tray. And for someone whose circle holds few businesses, or who does not want to do this work at all, the 1996 letter names a default. It says most investors will likely do best by owning a low-fee index fund. That is a description of what the letter says, not advice to buy any fund.

LiveWhy passing costs nothing3 min▶

An owner who passes on a business they do not understand loses nothing they had. They do not risk money on a guess, they do not spend weeks defending a view they cannot check, and they keep their attention for businesses they can judge. Nobody forces you to have an opinion on every business that crosses your path. There is no penalty for leaving one alone.

The pressure to act comes from elsewhere: a friend's enthusiasm, a story everyone is repeating, the sense that everyone else understands something you do not. The tray is the answer to all of them. "Too hard for me" is a complete sentence. It is also usually the correct one, because for any single person the businesses outside the circle vastly outnumber those inside it. Owner session 6 returns to why the urge to act feels so strong when the cost of not acting is zero.

Self-studyThe other kind of mistake: passing on what you did understand3 min▶

Passing is free when a business is outside your circle. It is not free when the business is inside it and the case is plain. In his 1989 letter, in a section on the mistakes of Berkshire's first twenty-five years, Buffett names mistakes of omission: failing to act on opportunities that were clearly within his competence. They never show up in any record of losses, which is why they are easy to ignore and, by his account, among the costliest.

So the too-hard tray has a counterpart rule. If a business is inside your circle, the four answers are written, Owner session 2's range is drawn and the margin in Owner session 3 is wide, then hesitating is a choice with a cost too. The circle is not an excuse to never decide. It tells you where deciding is your job and where it is not.

Real world

The 1996 letter's honest default. In the same letter that sets out the circle of competence, Buffett writes that most investors will likely do best owning a low-fee index fund. It is a striking thing to read from someone whose career was valuing businesses one at a time. The circle is a tool for people who want to do the work and know where their knowledge ends. For people who do not, the letter does not tell them to try harder; it names a simpler path. Recording what the letter says is not a recommendation of any fund or product.

Build-along

Draw your own circle

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

No code and no market prices. You need paper, a pen and ten businesses you know something about: where you work, places you shop, firms friends or relatives run, companies whose products you use. Halden Tiles is placed for you as the worked example; it is constructed, so its answers are invented, and your placements will differ from anyone else's. That difference is the point.

Step 1 · List ten businesses you know. Not the most famous ones: the ones you have seen from close up. Your employer, a former employer, a shop you use weekly, a firm a relative runs, a supplier you have dealt with at work. Write them down before you judge any of them.

★ Step 1 · the list1 Halden Tiles (constructed example) 2 ______________________ 3 ______________________ ... 10 ______________________

Step 2 · Run the four-question test on each. One line per question. Tick it only if you could explain the answer to a friend without looking anything up. A guess you would need to check gets a question mark.

★ Step 2 · Halden, constructedQ1 how it makes money: tick buys from four importers, sells to builders Q2 customers, why they return: tick small builders; staff advice and credit terms Q3 spend to stand still: ? 0.50 million, but only the cousin reads the books Q4 what breaks it in five years: ? direct online ordering; two builders already do

Step 3 · Place each one on the three rings. Four ticks: inside. One or two question marks: on the edge. Three or four, or a blank first line: outside. Be strict; the edge band is where generosity costs most.

★ Step 3 · placement rule4 ticks inside 1 or 2 question marks edge 3 or 4, or Q1 blank outside, too-hard tray Halden: 2 ticks, 2 marks edge

Step 4 · Pick the edge business closest to the inside. The one whose question marks look most learnable. If several tie, pick the one you would most enjoy studying; you will need the stamina.

★ Step 4 · closest to the insideClosest edge business: ______________________ Why this one: its open questions look learnable soon, not unknowable

Step 5 · Write what you would need to learn, and how. For each question mark: what would turn it into a tick, where you would find it, and whether it is learnable soon, learnable slowly, or not knowable from here. If every mark is the third kind, move the business to the too-hard tray now.

★ Step 5 · Halden's to-learn list, constructedQ3: ask for five years of accounts; check refits and vans against the 0.50 how: read the statements (Finance: Reading the Numbers) kind: learnable soon Q4: ask six builders how they order now and why how: conversations, not documents kind: learnable slowly, maybe never fully

Step 6 · Write your too-hard list. Every business that landed outside, plus any edge business whose questions are unknowable from where you stand. Read it once and notice how little it costs you to leave every one of them alone.

★ Step 6 · the too-hard listToo hard for me, for now: ______________________ reason: cannot say how it earns ______________________ reason: what breaks it is not knowable from here Cost of passing on all of them: nothing I already had

Keep the circle as a standing rule. Before any valuation, run the four questions and place the business. Only an inside business gets a value range. An edge business gets a to-learn list. An outside business gets the tray. Redraw the circle once a year; it should grow slowly, and if it grew fast, check whether you learned or just got familiar.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Owner session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In the sense of Buffett's 1996 letter, what does it mean for a business to be inside your circle of competence?

Familiarity is not competence; loving a product answers part of one question at most. The letter's weight is on knowing the boundary, and the boundary is drawn by what you can explain, not what you recognise. Price plays no part in placing a business.

2 · Halden Tiles (constructed) passes two of the four questions and leaves two as question marks. What is the honest next move?

An edge business is not a no, and not a yes. A margin of safety covers errors in an estimate you are able to make; it cannot cover not knowing what the business is worth to begin with. Halden's books are learnable soon; how fast builders move online may never be fully knowable, and that can be reason enough to pass.

3 · Besides the circle of competence, what does the 1996 letter say about most investors?

The letter names the low-fee index fund as the likely best path for most investors. Recording that is describing the letter, not recommending any fund. The circle is for people who want to do the work; the letter does not tell everyone else to try harder.
